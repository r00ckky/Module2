The Manhattan Project

The Manhattan Project was a research and development undertaking during World War II that produced the first nuclear weapons. It was led by the United States with the support of the United Kingdom and Canada. From 1942 to 1946, the project was under the direction of Major General Leslie Groves of the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. The Army component of the project was designated the Manhattan District; Manhattan gradually superseded the official codename, Development of Substitute Materials, as the project's title. The project absorbed its earlier British counterpart, Tube Alloys.

The Manhattan Project began modestly in 1939, but grew to employ more than 130,000 people and cost nearly US$2 billion (equivalent to about $24 billion in 2023). Over 90% of the cost was for building factories and to produce fissile material, with less than 10% for development and production of the weapons. Research and production took place at more than 30 sites across the United States, the United Kingdom, and Canada.

Two types of atomic bombs were developed concurrently during the war. A uranium gun-type fission weapon was given the codename "Little Boy" and a plutonium implosion-type nuclear weapon was called "Fat Man". The project also had a smaller research programme aimed at developing a nuclear reactor, which culminated in the first artificial nuclear reactor, Chicago Pile-1, going critical on December 2, 1942, in a racquetball court at the University of Chicago under the stands of Stagg Field.

The scientific work was led by American physicist J. Robert Oppenheimer. Other notable scientists involved included Enrico Fermi, who achieved the first nuclear chain reaction, Niels Bohr, the Danish physicist who contributed theoretical insights, and Hungarian-American physicist Leo Szilard, who had originally conceived the nuclear chain reaction. Klaus Fuchs, a German-born British theoretical physicist, worked on the project but was later revealed to be a Soviet spy who had passed atomic secrets to the USSR.

The project resulted in the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki in Japan on August 6 and 9, 1945 respectively. The Hiroshima bomb, Little Boy, killed an estimated 70,000–80,000 people immediately, with tens of thousands more dying later from radiation exposure. The Nagasaki bomb, Fat Man, killed an estimated 40,000 people immediately. The bombings led to Japan's unconditional surrender on August 15, 1945, ending World War II.

The Trinity test, conducted on July 16, 1945, at the Alamogordo Bombing and Gunnery Range in New Mexico, was the first detonation of a nuclear device. J. Robert Oppenheimer, upon witnessing the explosion, reportedly recalled a verse from the Hindu scripture Bhagavad Gita: "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds."

After the war, the Manhattan Project was succeeded by the Atomic Energy Commission, which took over from the Army in January 1947. The project left a complex legacy: it demonstrated the potential of large-scale government investment in science and technology, but also initiated the nuclear arms race between the United States and the Soviet Union that defined much of the Cold War era.

The project was carried out at several key sites. Los Alamos Laboratory in New Mexico served as the primary weapons design facility. Oak Ridge, Tennessee, housed uranium enrichment facilities. Hanford Site in Washington state was used for plutonium production. The Metallurgical Laboratory at the University of Chicago conducted much of the early nuclear research.

Several scientists involved in the project later expressed moral reservations. Albert Einstein, whose 1939 letter to President Franklin D. Roosevelt had helped initiate the project, later stated he regretted signing it. Oppenheimer himself became an advocate for international control of nuclear weapons and opposed the development of the hydrogen bomb, which led to the revocation of his security clearance in 1954.
